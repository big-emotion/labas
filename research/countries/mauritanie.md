## Country at a glance (for someone moving, not visiting)

- **Languages used day to day**: Arabic (Hassaniya dialect in the street) dominates. Pulaar, Soninké and Wolof are national languages, and French is still widely used in business and administration. Expat.com lists Arabic, French, Pulaar, Soninké and Wolof [3, 2018, flagged old]. The 2010 nationality law dropped French as a language requirement for naturalisation [6]. That is a signal that French's official standing is shrinking, even though daily use remains.
- **Currency**: ouguiya (MRU), the only legal tender since 1 July 2018. **Shops do not accept bank cards or cheques**, so cash is king. Some bank branches have ATMs [2, updated 2026-11-09]. Mobile banking exists: Bankily, from Banque Populaire de Mauritanie, handles transfers, merchant payments, SOMELEC (power) and SNDE (water) bills, and USSD *888# [12, undated].
- **Entry and residence for French/EU passport holders**: since 5 January 2025, entry takes two steps. First, an e-visa authorisation on anrpts.gov.mr before boarding. Second, a visa issued at the border with biometrics, at **€55 for 30 days, single entry**. Overstays must be regularised before exit, at the same cost. Holders of a valid *carte de séjour* (resident card) who have lived in the country more than three months do not need a visa for trips, as long as they have not been away more than 6 months. Visa extensions go through the ANRPTS office in Tevragh Zeina, Nouakchott. There is no specific business visa [1, 2026-11-09; 13, 2026-11-09]. How to get a residence card: **not found**.
- **Diaspora with family roots**: dual nationality has been allowed since Law 2010-023 (11 Feb 2010). However, a Mauritanian of origin can lose nationality by taking another one "without the permission of the authorities". Nationality passes automatically through the father, wherever the child is born. Through the mother, it passes automatically only if the child is born in Mauritania and the father is foreign, stateless or unknown. A child born abroad to a Mauritanian mother and a foreign father can only register in the year before reaching majority. Naturalisation normally requires 10 years of residence, or 5 for some cases including spouses. Applicants must speak Arabic, Pulaar, Soninké or Wolof [6]. **No diaspora visa or right-of-return scheme was found.** Note that many Afro-Mauritanians have had trouble with civil registration (see Legal & sensitive context) [11].
- **Laws that shape daily life**: alcohol and pork are illegal for Muslims and non-Muslims alike, and luggage is scanned on arrival. During Ramadan, do not eat, drink or smoke in public in daytime, even in a car. Dress modestly: women cover their shoulders and avoid short skirts, and men avoid shorts and sleeveless tops [5, FCDO, 2026]. Photographing public, military or religious buildings, ports, airports or officials can lead to prosecution [2].

## Cities

### Nouakchott

Capital, 1,446,761 inhabitants (2023 census). It has nine communes: Tevragh Zeina, Ksar and Sebkha (West); Arafat, El Mina and Riyad (South); Dar Naïm, Teyarett and Toujounine (North) [7].

- **Cost of living**: Expat.com's guide gives about **€800/month for one person and €2,300 for a family of four**, but that figure dates from 2018 [3]. The only rent figures found are forum posts from about 2008 to 2022: an "average apartment" at about 35,000 old ouguiya, roughly €92 at the time, and a two-storey villa at about 600,000 old ouguiya, roughly €1,580 [4]. These are too old to use. **No 2023–2026 rent data was found.** Numbeo did not respond (HTTP 429) [—]. Expat forum threads about housing ("Quel type de logement Nouakchott", "quartier Français à Nouakchott?") show that people mainly ask about Tevragh Zeina and "Ilot K" [9]. That Tevragh Zeina is the expat area is a low-confidence inference from Expat.com thread titles and the location of the ANRPTS office.
- **Work & money**: the city is the country's economic centre and hosts the headquarters of the national banks. About 90% of activity was informal (1999 figure) [7]. Other employers: AFD, AfDB, the IMF office and the French Treasury's economic service [13]. National sectors are iron ore (SNIM), gold, fishing and offshore gas (GTA field shared with Senegal) [8]. Expat.com says expatriation is driven by service-sector jobs [3, 2018]. Salaries: **not found** (only an outdated forum anecdote: a public-school teacher earned about €400/month, ca. 2022 [4]). Remote work depends on internet quality, which was **not found**.
- **Entrepreneurship & tech**: **not found** in this session (no TechCabal or Disrupt Africa page could be reached). Fintech exists through bank-led mobile wallets such as Bankily [12]. Crypto adoption, incubators and investors: **not found**. Company registration: **not found**.
- **Daily infrastructure**: three mobile operators: Mauritel (Maroc Telecom), Mattel (Tunisie Telecom) and Chinguitel [10]. Wikipedia's figures are outdated. Bills for power (SOMELEC) and water (SNDE) can be paid through Bankily [12]. The Sheikh Zayed solar plant was completed in 2012 [7]. Belgium's foreign ministry says heavy city-centre traffic and local driving habits call for particular care [14, 2026-05-20]. Canada's view of public transport was seen only in a search snippet (unverified). Car hire usually comes with a driver [2]. Ride-hailing apps: **not found**. Frequency of power cuts: **not found**. Parts of the city lie below sea level and the coast floods [7].
- **Health & schools**: there are public hospitals, private clinics and good pharmacies, but medicine supply is irregular and ambulances are very limited. Private facilities are better equipped. After stabilisation, medical evacuation abroad is advised, so take insurance that covers repatriation [15, 2026-11-09]. Malaria occurs in Nouakchott, mostly during or after the August–November rainy season. Dengue and chikungunya are present [15]. Schools: **Lycée français Théodore Monod** (lftm-mr.net), primary to lycée, uses AEFE tools [16, 17]. The **Institut français de Mauritanie** is also there [17]. Names of reference hospitals: **not found**.
- **Safety**: crime is "relatively low compared with cities of similar size". However, violent thefts by motorcyclists are rising, including in good neighbourhoods, and crime is growing on the outskirts. On match or concert days at the Olympic Stadium, gangs sometimes carry knives, so avoid walking there at night. Women face a risk of assault in isolated places such as beaches and outlying districts. Nouakchott is in the **yellow zone** (heightened vigilance) [18, updated 2026-11-09; zones 2026-03-11]. The UK says crime is "moderate but steadily increasing". It mentions burglaries where foreign nationals live, daylight robberies, and knife-point robberies on isolated beaches, and advises particular care in the Le Cinquième, Dar Naïm and Cité Plage districts. Terrorism "cannot be ruled out" [5, FCDO, last updated 2026-10-19]. The US level 3 "Reconsider Travel" rating and the daytime-only rule for US staff leaving Nouakchott come from a July 2025 search snippet; the page returned 403 again on 2026-11-14 (unverified) [19].
- **Social life & vibe**: no alcohol by law, so social life happens in homes, cafés and restaurants and around tea [2, 5]. The Sebkha district has a large shopping area [7]. Tioulit beach near the airport is mentioned by expats [4]. Religion structures daily rhythm, Ramadan in particular [5]. Music, sport and nightlife sources: **not found**.
- **Diaspora & expat community**: about 50,000 expatriates, mainly Senegalese, Malians, Moroccans, French and Chinese [3, 2018]. The Expat.com Mauritania forum is active but small, with threads from 2–5 years ago [9]. The French embassy recommends registering with the consulate for stays over 6 months [17]. Facebook/WhatsApp groups and returnee associations: **not found** in this session.
- **Climate in practice**: hot desert climate, with highs around 33 °C all year and winter nights sometimes below 10 °C. About 113 mm of rain falls a year, mostly July–September. September–October is the hottest period [7]. France Diplomatie says April–October is very hot, often above 40 °C [2]. Dust and sand come in from the eastern dunes [7].
- **Watch out**: cash-only economy [2]; no legal alcohol [5]; healthcare that requires evacuation for anything serious [15]; rising street crime [18, 5]; flood-prone low areas [7]; and treatment of Black West African migrants and Afro-Mauritanians (see below) [11, 20].

### Nouadhibou

Second city, 173,525 inhabitants (2023 census), on the Cap Blanc peninsula next to Western Sahara, 450 km from Nouakchott by the RN2 road [21].

- **Cost of living**: **not found** (no rent or price data).
- **Work & money**: fishing is the main activity, and iron ore processing the largest industry. The port opened in 1966. The SNIM railway runs 670 km to the Zouérate mines [21]. Wikipedia does not mention the free-zone status, so it is **not confirmed** here. Salaries: **not found**.
- **Entrepreneurship & tech**: **not found**.
- **Daily infrastructure**: an airport, the RN2 to Nouakchott, and the SNIM ore train, which has passenger cars. Riding on the ore wagons is forbidden and dangerous [2, 21]. In October 2026 the FCDO added new information on the risks of the iron ore train [5]. Power, internet and ride-hailing: **not found**.
- **Health & schools**: **not found** for the city. A claim that care outside Nouakchott is limited came from a search snippet; neither the France Diplomatie security page [18] nor its health page [15] says so when opened on 2026-11-14 (unverified).
- **Safety**: the UK advises against all but essential travel within 25 km of the Western Sahara border, but explicitly excludes the city of Nouadhibou and the Nouakchott–Nouadhibou road [5, 2026-10-19]. France puts the stretch from Nouadhibou to Zouérate in the orange zone, except the PK55 crossing; the edges of the PK55 track are mined [18]. No city-specific crime data was found.
- **Social life & vibe**: a fishing and port town. It has the "ship graveyard" (300+ wrecks), ties to Saint-Exupéry, and a meteorite trade [21]. Otherwise **not found**.
- **Diaspora & expat community**: it is a major departure point for migrants heading to the Canary Islands [21]. HRW (Aug 2025) documented abuses in migration control [20]. Expat groups: **not found**.
- **Climate in practice**: desert but mild because of the sea, averaging 21.3 °C with about 18–23 mm of rain a year (the article's text and climate table disagree) [21]. That is noticeably cooler than Nouakchott.
- **Watch out**: isolation (450 km from the capital), healthcare not documented and likely thinner than in the capital (unverified), heavy police focus on migration [20], and a one-industry economy [21].

*Other cities*: the sources reached did not support a sourced section for any other city. Atar (Adrar, gateway to the desert) and Rosso (on the border, the crossing to Senegal) would be the next to research. Zouérate is the mining town and lies near FCDO/France Diplomatie restricted zones [5, 18].

## Social atmosphere through a Big Five lens (interpretive, editorial)

These are editorial readings of cited facts. They do not measure anyone's personality. No Hofstede or GLOBE data for Mauritania was found in this session.

**Nouakchott**
- Openness: suits people curious about Saharan, Arab-Berber and West African cultures living side by side (Hassaniya, Pulaar, Soninké, Wolof [3, 6]). It is less suited to people who need a broad cultural or night scene, since alcohol is banned and nightlife sources were not found [5].
- Conscientiousness: an advantage. You have to plan for cash [2], the e-visa and residence formalities [1], and private insurance with evacuation cover [15].
- Extraversion: social life happens in homes and at tea rather than in bars [5]. Quieter, home-centred people adapt more easily than people who live for going out.
- Agreeableness: a dress code [5], the Ramadan code [5] and photography restrictions [2] reward people who accept local social rules without friction.
- Emotional stability (stress tolerance): heat above 40 °C from April to October [2], aggressive traffic [14], rising street crime [18] and thin emergency care [15] call for good stress tolerance.

**Nouadhibou**
- Openness: life is shaped by the port and fishing, with few cultural venues documented [21].
- Extraversion: a smaller, more isolated town [21]. It suits people who are content with a small circle.
- Emotional stability: distance from the capital and undocumented healthcare (unverified) call for self-reliance. The milder climate [21] removes one stressor.

Afrobarometer Round 10 (2025): 52% would like or not mind immigrant or foreign-worker neighbours, 68% oppose immigrants marrying into their family, and 64% oppose free movement for work in West Africa [22]. This hints at a cautious attitude toward foreigners that newcomers should expect.

## Legal & sensitive context (each with a dated source)

- **LGBT**: same-sex acts are illegal. Under the 1983 penal code (art. 308), the death penalty applies in theory to Muslim men. It has not been applied since 1986, and an unofficial moratorium was confirmed in 2021. Women face 3 months to 2 years in prison [23]. In January 2020, ten men were arrested after a birthday-party video; HRW covered it on 2020-02-07 [11, 23]. In 2025, two men were arrested after a video of them kissing went viral [23]. The FCDO notes there are no anti-discrimination protections [5, 2026]. **Not a safe country to live openly as LGBT.**
- **Racism / descent-based discrimination**: according to HRW's country page (undated summary), some forms of slavery persist despite laws and special courts. HRW has reported on repression of anti-discrimination activists (2018-02-12) and on civil-registration barriers that keep children out of school (2018-03-29) [11]. These issues mainly affect Haratine and Afro-Mauritanian communities; that detail is editorial context, so confirm it with a newer source. On **2025-08-27**, HRW published "They Accused Me of Trying to Go to Europe", a report on abuses in migration control linked to EU externalisation [11]. That is directly relevant to Black sub-Saharan Africans living in Nouakchott and Nouadhibou. **Diaspora readers from West Africa should read that report before moving.**
- **Conflict zones**: the border with Mali and the east are red or "advise against all travel" zones. The jihadist threat spills over from the Sahel, with a risk of kidnapping or attack. The Western Sahara border is restricted, except Nouadhibou and the coastal road. Assaba, Gorgol, Guidimaka and the Hodhs are orange or "all but essential" [5, 2026-10-19; 18, 2026-11-09]. Both cities in the product are outside these zones.
- **Women's daily experience**: risk of assault in isolated places such as beaches and outlying districts, so avoid going alone, especially at night [18, 2026]. A law on violence against women, drafted in 2016, had not passed when HRW last noted it (undated summary) [11]. HRW's 2018-09-05 report "They Told Me to Keep Quiet" documents obstacles to justice for sexual-assault survivors [11]. Afrobarometer (2026-04-21 dispatch, 2025-07-11 release): most Mauritanians think men have more right to jobs than women, though most women disagree [22]. Dress code for women [5].
- **Freedom of the press**: foreign journalists need prior authorisation, and some have been arrested or expelled [2, 2026-11-09].
- **Freedom of belief**: on 2020-10-19 HRW reported activists held for eight months on blasphemy charges, facing possible death sentences [11]. Keep religious criticism off public social media.
