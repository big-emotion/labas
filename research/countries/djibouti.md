## Country at a glance (for someone moving, not visiting)

- **Languages used day to day:** French and Arabic are the official languages. French is the language of the administration, business and public schooling [8][9]. Somali (Issa) and Afar are the home and street languages of the two largest groups [10]. English is useful around the foreign bases and the port. Not verified by a dedicated source.
- **Religion:** more than 90 % Sunni Muslim, and fewer than 3 % Christian [10].
- **Currency:** Djiboutian franc (DJF). It has been pegged to the US dollar since 1949 through a currency board, at **177.721 DJF = 1 USD** [11]. Rough conversion used in this dossier: 1 EUR ≈ 205 DJF. That rate is my own estimate from the USD peg and an assumed EUR/USD of about 1.15, and it was not checked against a live rate. Leases are often priced in USD or EUR [2].
- **Population:** the capital had 776,966 residents at the 2024 census, which is the large majority of the country [9].
- **(a) French/EU passport holders:** a visa is required. Options are an e-visa at evisa.gouv.dj or a tourist visa on arrival, paid in cash in USD. You need proof of onward travel and a passport valid for 6 months [4]. Fees were not found. To work, you need a work permit from ANEFIP, which costs 50,000, 100,000 or 200,000 DJF per year depending on the job category [3]. The long-stay residence-card procedure and its cost were **not found**.
- **(b) Diaspora with family roots:** under the Nationality Code (Law 200/AN/1981, revised in 2004), a child born anywhere to at least one parent who is Djiboutian by birth is Djiboutian. **Dual nationality has been allowed since the 2004 revision**, though not for the president. Naturalisation normally takes 10 years of residence. For a spouse it takes 10 years of marriage, or 5 if the couple has children. The code is now Articles 26–64 of the 2018 Civil Code [12]. **No diaspora visa or right-of-return scheme was found** comparable to those of Ghana or Benin.

## Cities

### Djibouti (Djibouti-ville)

- **Cost of living:** this is among the most expensive cities in Africa, because almost everything is imported [3].
  - A 1-bedroom in the centre costs **500–800 USD/month (≈ 89,000–142,000 DJF, ≈ €435–700)**, and 300–500 USD on the outskirts. A 3-bedroom in the centre costs 1,200–1,800 USD, and a villa in a residential area 1,500–3,500 USD. The deposit is 1–3 months, and utilities are rarely included in the rent (Africarrières, Feb 2026) [2].
  - Expat.com gives 150,000–250,000 DJF for a 2-bedroom in the centre, and 1,500–2,500 USD for family flats in the sought-after areas [3].
  - **Neighbourhoods:**
    - **Les Hérons:** the most popular area for expatriate families. It is calm and greener, with gated residences. A 3-bedroom villa there costs 300,000–450,000 DJF/month (Expat.com, Nov 2025) [3].
    - **Hérouale:** near the airport and embassies, a family alternative with villas up to 400,000 DJF/month [3].
    - **Hamoudi and Plateau du Serpent:** "slightly cheaper" [2].
    - **City centre:** lively and noisy [3].
    - **Newer outer districts:** cheaper, but you need a car [3].
    - **Balbala:** a large suburb [9]. Its standing was not documented in the sources opened.
  - **Food:** a cheap local meal costs 5–8 USD, and a mid-range restaurant meal 12–20 USD. Food for one person costs 300–500 USD/month [2].
  - **Monthly budget:** Expat.com, citing Numbeo for April 2025, gives about 510,900 DJF/month for a single person. That is about 2,875 USD. The site's own EUR conversion is inconsistent [3]. A modest local lifestyle is far cheaper, but no reliable figure was found.
- **Work & money:**
  - **Main employers:** the state, the Port of Djibouti, the airport and the foreign military bases (French, US, Chinese, Japanese and Italian), plus embassies [3]. The US base is Camp Lemonnier. China's base near Doraleh opened in 2017 [11].
  - The economy is about 82 % services: the port, transshipment and the Ethiopia corridor [11]. Growth was 7.0 % in 2024 and an estimated 6.5 % in 2025, and is forecast at 5.7 % in 2026 because of Red Sea and Hormuz disruption (World Bank, 2026) [7].
  - **Unemployment:** the official rate is just over 10 %, a 2017 estimate gives 40 %, and international estimates run near 60 %. Youth unemployment was 76.8 % in 2025 [11].
  - **Salaries:** the average net salary is about 505,700 DJF/month. There is a public-sector minimum of 45,000 DJF and no legal private-sector minimum [3].
  - **Remote work:** feasible, given the monopoly operator and the costs below. Not documented.
- **Entrepreneurship & tech:**
  - **Company registration:** online for some sectors through ODPIC. The DDID free-trade zone is the main route for import-export [3].
  - The World Bank's 2026 Country Private Sector Diagnostic and a 25.75 M USD economic-diversification programme mention digital infrastructure as an opportunity [7].
  - **Startup hubs, incubators, investor presence and mobile-money / crypto adoption:** **not found** in the sources I could open. Not checked: the D-Money / Waafi wallets (dmoney.dj did not resolve on 2026-11-14) and any local incubators. No crypto claim should be made. Treat as a gap.
- **Daily infrastructure:**
  - **Power:** about 120 MW of thermal plants plus imported Ethiopian hydro, which does not always meet demand [11]. The Ghoubet wind farm (about 59–60 MW) has run since late 2023: Wikipedia gives commercial commissioning on 10 Sept 2023, the World Bank says operating since December 2023. The target is 100 % renewable by 2035 [7][20]. Air-conditioning electricity is expensive: 40–80 USD/month for a 2-bedroom [2][3].
  - **Internet and mobile:** Djibouti Telecom is a state monopoly. 4G+ has existed since 2017 [13]. Home internet costs 30–60 USD/month and a mobile plan 10–25 USD [2]. The city is a regional submarine-cable hub [13]. Fibre availability for homes was not found.
  - **Water:** tap water is not drinkable, and water costs 15–30 USD/month [2][3].
  - **Transport:** a car is usually needed outside the centre [3]. Driving standards are poor and khat contributes to dangerous driving (FCDO) [5]. The Addis Ababa railway runs from Nagad station [9], and the airport is 6 km from the centre [9].
  - **Ride-hailing apps:** not found.
- **Health & schools:**
  - **Health:** Hôpital Général Peltier is the main public hospital. Private clinics such as the Polyclinique offer more comfort at higher prices. International insurance with evacuation cover is strongly recommended, and specialised medicines can be hard to find [3].
  - **Quality School International:** American curriculum, about 150 pupils, fees of 13,400–16,200 USD/year in 2024–25 [3].
  - **International School of Africa:** English-language [3].
  - **International School of Djibouti:** bilingual French-English [3].
  - **Local public schools:** follow the French model and teach in French [3][10].
  - **Lycée Kessel:** a French lycée exists, but this is unverified because the page could not be fetched. Check it on aefe.fr.
- **Safety:**
  - **France Diplomatie** (page of 15 Sept 2026, map of 5 Mar 2026): the city is in the **yellow** "enhanced vigilance" zone. There has been no attack in the city since 24 May 2014. It recommends vigilance in places frequented by expatriates [1].
  - **Red zones:** the Eritrean border (land, sea and air), the Somaliland border area past the "Refuge Décan" on the road to Loyada, and the Sept-Frères (Sawabi) archipelago except through authorised agency trips. Oued Kalou and Mont Moussa Ali, in the army-controlled north, are closed to visitors (France Diplomatie, 15 Sept 2026) [1].
  - **At sea:** navigation in the southern Red Sea and Bab-el-Mandeb is advised against indefinitely, as of 5 Nov 2026 [1b].
  - **FCDO** (update date 27 July 2026 recorded by the first pass; the page re-read on 2026-11-14 still says the same things): terrorist attacks are likely, and Al-Shabaab has threatened Djibouti. Petty crime is the risk, so do not walk alone late at night. **Photographing ports, the airport and military sites can lead to arrest** [5][6].
  - **Neighbourhood-level nuance:** not found.
- **Social life & vibe:**
  - Nightlife is modest: a few live-music bars and cafés [3].
  - **Food:** skoudehkaris (spiced rice), lahoh pancakes, grilled fish, lamb stews [3].
  - **Outdoors:** diving and snorkelling in the Gulf of Tadjourah, with whale sharks from November to January. Kitesurfing, seafront running, and Lake Assal and Lake Abbé nearby [3].
  - Islam shapes the calendar: Eid holidays, with Independence Day on 27 June [3]. Khat chewing is part of afternoon social life (FCDO mentions khat) [5].
- **Diaspora & expat community:**
  - The Expat.com Djibouti forum has about 2,700 members [3].
  - The large military and diplomatic communities form their own networks [3].
  - **Diaspora / returnee associations, Facebook or WhatsApp groups:** not found. This needs a social-media pass.
- **Climate in practice:**
  - Average highs are 30 °C from December to February and 39–41 °C from June to August. There is almost no rain: about 113 mm a year, with March–May and November the "wettest" [14].
  - Summer heat above 40 °C from May to September pushes activity to early morning and evening [3]. Wind peaks in July and August [14].
- **Watch out:**
  - High cost of living, with imported goods and expensive air-conditioning [3].
  - Telecom monopoly [13].
  - Very high youth unemployment [11].
  - Authoritarian political climate, with no media independence (Freedom House 2025: 24/100, "Not Free") [15].
  - Strict ban on photographing infrastructure [5].
  - Extreme summer heat [14].

### Tadjourah

- **Profile:** about 18,808 inhabitants (2024 census) on the north shore of the Gulf of Tadjourah. It is a historic port with whitewashed houses and mosques, and Rimbaud lived there in the 1880s [16].
- **Getting there:** a ferry to the capital takes about 2.5 hours. The road is about 130 km, and the RN-11 runs to Balho. There is an airstrip, with a flight of about 30 minutes [16].
- **Cost of living:** **not found.** No rental data was found. Expat.com says secondary towns lack housing to expatriate standards [3].
- **Work & money:** a new port of about 90 M USD was built, mainly for general cargo such as livestock, sesame and grain. It was first meant to ship potash from Ethiopia's Afar Region [16]. Outside that, jobs are mostly in the public sector, fishing and small trade. That last point is unverified.
- **Entrepreneurship & tech:** not found. Realistically this means remote work using the capital's ecosystem.
- **Daily infrastructure:** not found specifically for this town. Nationally, rural electrification is very low (1 % rural against 54 % urban in 2020) [11]. Tadjourah is a town, so it is probably better served than that, but this is unverified.
- **Health & schools:** there are no international schools or private clinics outside the capital (Expat.com) [3]. Serious care means going to Djibouti-ville.
- **Safety:** the town is in France Diplomatie's yellow zone. North of the Balho–Adaïlou–Ouaddhi–Godoria axis, army checkpoints apply and access may need authorisation [1]. The Eritrean border to the north is red [1][5].
- **Social life & vibe:** quiet, conservative, mostly Afar, with beaches nearby [16]. There is no nightlife to speak of (inferred).
- **Diaspora & expat community:** not found.
- **Climate:** arid, with about 186 mm of rain a year and average temperatures of 26 °C in winter and 36 °C in summer [16].
- **Watch out:** isolation from health care, and limited services and housing stock [3]. Near the sensitive northern zone [1].

### Ali Sabieh (additional: second city, cooler upland)

- **Why it matters:** with 44,782 inhabitants (2024) it is the country's second-largest city. At 756 m it is cooler than the coast, with summer highs of 32–37 °C and winter lows of 15–16 °C [17].
- **Economy:** a stop on the Addis–Djibouti railway, with small industry tied to construction and some agriculture. Its regional hospital is the Dr. Absieh hospital [17].
- It shares the downsides of Tadjourah: no international schools or private clinics [3].
- **Cost of living and daily services:** not found.

## Social atmosphere through a Big Five lens (interpretive, editorial)

This section is editorial interpretation built from the facts above. No city "measures" a trait. **No academic study was opened:** Hofstede, GLOBE and Afrobarometer data for Djibouti were not found, and Djibouti is not a standard Afrobarometer round country as far as I could verify.

**Djibouti-ville**
- **Openness:** suits people curious about a crossroads city, with French, Somali, Afar, Arab, Ethiopian and military communities [3][9][11]. The cultural offer is thin, and night entertainment is modest [3].
- **Conscientiousness:** suits planners. You need a work permit before arrival [3], a relocation package, and a school application months ahead [3]. The heat calendar also needs organising [14].
- **Extraversion:** social life runs through small circles, such as the expat forum, embassies, bases, diving and kite clubs [3]. It suits people who are fine with a small, repeated social network rather than a big-city nightlife.
- **Agreeableness:** social codes are conservative and religious [10]. Political talk and photography are sensitive, with arrests possible [5][15]. Discretion and adaptability help.
- **Emotional stability (stress tolerance):** you need tolerance for heat above 40 °C [14], high prices [3], a background terror threat [5] and regional instability at sea [1b].

**Tadjourah / Ali Sabieh**
- **Openness:** suits people drawn to a slow, traditional small-town life [16][17].
- **Extraversion:** suits introverts or remote workers who value quiet [3].
- **Conscientiousness:** you need to be self-reliant about health care and supplies [3].
- **Agreeableness:** you need to fit into a close-knit, conservative community (inferred).
- **Emotional stability (stress tolerance):** you need to cope with isolation. Ali Sabieh's altitude makes the climate easier [17].

## Legal & sensitive context (each with a dated source)

- **LGBT:**
  - According to the FCDO (updated July 2026), same-sex activity is "not illegal". However, public affection can attract police attention under "good morals" laws [5].
  - Wikipedia (consulted 2026-11-14) reports a **conflict between sources**: the Human Dignity Trust says it is criminalised, with up to 3 years in prison. There is no recognition of couples, no anti-discrimination protection and no legal gender change [18].
  - Wikipedia's article text calls it "de facto illegal" (citing Outright International), while its own summary table marks it legal, so the page contradicts itself [18]. Freedom House 2025 scores the question on political rights for groups including LGBT+ people at 1/4 [15].
  - Treat the country as **hostile in practice; legal status disputed**. The primary penal code text was not opened, and the Human Dignity Trust profile returned 404 again on 2026-11-14.
- **Political freedom:**
  - Freedom House 2025 scores Djibouti 24/100, "Not Free", with media independence at 0/4. Critics of President Guelleh, in power since 1999 with no term limits, are harassed or arrested [15].
- **Women:**
  - Female genital mutilation (FGM) among girls aged 14 and under fell from 43 % to 31 % between 2012 and 2019 (Freedom House 2025) [15].
  - Women face significant barriers to the labour market (World Bank, 2026) [7].
  - Specific reports on daily street harassment: not found.
- **Racism, colourism and treatment of diaspora or foreigners:** **no reliable source found.** The US State Department human-rights report (403) could not be opened. Not covered: the treatment of Ethiopian migrants and Afar–Issa ethnic tensions are known topics, but they are not documented here.
- **Conflict zones:**
  - The Eritrean border is red for France [1] and advised against by the FCDO [5].
  - The Somaliland border beyond Loyada is red [1].
  - At sea: Red Sea and Bab-el-Mandeb military activity, and rising Somali piracy (France Diplomatie, 5 Nov 2026) [1b].
  - Terrorism: Al-Shabaab threats (FCDO 2026) [5].
